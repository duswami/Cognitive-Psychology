# Behavioral Psychology

Behavioral psychology (behaviorism) studies observable behavior and how it is shaped by the environment through conditioning. It focuses on what can be measured objectively rather than internal mental states.

## Key Principles
- All behavior is learned through interaction with the environment.
- Behavior is shaped by antecedents (triggers) and consequences.
- Reinforcement increases the likelihood of a behavior; punishment decreases it.

## Classical Conditioning (Pavlov)
- A neutral stimulus is paired with an unconditioned stimulus that naturally elicits a response.
- After repeated pairing, the neutral stimulus becomes a conditioned stimulus that elicits a conditioned response.
- Example: Bell (neutral) + food (unconditioned stimulus) → salivation (unconditioned response). Eventually bell alone → salivation.

## Operant Conditioning (Skinner)
- Focuses on voluntary behaviors and their consequences.
- Based on Thorndike's Law of Effect: behaviors followed by satisfying outcomes are repeated; those followed by unpleasant outcomes are not.
- Reinforcement: positive (add desirable) or negative (remove aversive).
- Punishment: positive (add aversive) or negative (remove desirable).
- Schedules of reinforcement (fixed/variable ratio/interval) affect rate and persistence of behavior.

## Key Figures
- Ivan Pavlov: classical conditioning
- John B. Watson: founded behaviorism, emphasized observable behavior
- B.F. Skinner: operant conditioning, radical behaviorism
- Edward Thorndike: Law of Effect
- Albert Bandura: social learning theory, observational learning, self-efficacy (bridge to cognitive)

## Applications
- Behavior modification and therapy (e.g., aversion therapy, token economies)
- Habit formation and breaking
- Education and training
- Animal training
